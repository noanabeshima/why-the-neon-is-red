There was a red tube on the wall and a question underneath it: what is that light, actually? Six answers, in the order you asked them.

What is light? What should I picture?

Noa — "I don't really know how to think of light"

Do not picture a little ball. Picture a field — a quantity that has a value at every point in empty space, the way temperature has a value at every point in a room. Space is not empty; it is filled with the electric and magnetic field, usually sitting quietly at zero.

Shake a charge and you put a kink in that field. The kink does not stay put: a changing electric field creates a magnetic field, and a changing magnetic field creates an electric one, so the disturbance keeps regenerating itself as it moves. That self-chasing pattern is light. Nothing material travels — only the pattern does, and it travels at exactly one speed, always.

But that picture is only half true, and the other half is the strange one. When light hands over its energy, it never hands over a little at a time. It arrives in indivisible lumps. Turn a source down and down and the lumps do not get gentler — they get rarer. One lands, or none does.

It travels like a spread-out wave and lands like a hail of pellets. Nothing you have ever held does both, which is why every mental image of a photon is wrong. The honest picture is: a ripple in a field, that can only be paid out in whole coins.

The coin has a fixed value set by the wavelength: short waves, expensive coins. Blue light delivers roughly twice the energy per photon as red. That single fact drives everything below.

Why does a gas glow in a few colours and a filament in all of them?

The pizza-place tube vs. the bulb over the counter

Because they are two completely different mechanisms wearing the same word, "light".

A hot filament glows because it is hot. Its atoms are jammed together, jostling, and every jostle radiates. There are no special energies in a jostle, so you get every wavelength at once — a smooth hill of colour whose peak slides from red to white as it gets hotter. This is thermal light, and it is the same physics as the sun and a campfire.

A neon tube is not hot. It is a nearly-empty glass pipe with a few thousand volts across it. Loose electrons get flung down the tube, slam into isolated neon atoms, and knock their electrons up onto higher shelves. An atom can only sit on specific shelves — that is what "quantised" means — so when the electron drops back down, the photon it emits carries exactly the gap between two shelves. Not approximately. Exactly.

The colour is the element. Neon's shelf spacings are neon's alone, so that orange-red is a fingerprint — you can read the gas off the wall from across the room.

This is not a metaphor for how we know what stars are made of. It is literally how we know: split starlight, look at which lines are there, name the elements. Helium was found in the sun's spectrum before anyone found it on Earth.

A dozen sharp lines??? Why a dozen in particular?

Noa

Nothing magic about twelve. It is a counting problem, and you can do the count.

Neon's visible glow comes from electrons falling between two crowded groups of levels. The upper group (called 2p) has 10 closely-spaced levels. The lower group (1s) has 4. Any upper can drop to any lower, which is 40 candidate jumps — but selection rules, the bookkeeping of angular momentum, forbid some, and 30 survive.

Of those thirty, some are far brighter than others, and some land outside what your eye can see. What is left — around a dozen strong lines, all crammed between 585 and 703 nm — blends in your retina into that one unmistakable orange-red.

So the dozen is not a law. It is what happens when a ten-level shelf sits above a four-level shelf. Different atom, different shelf structure, different number of lines: sodium is famous for essentially two (the yellow doublet at 589 nm), and iron has thousands.

Why emit one photon instead of two that sum to the same energy?

Noa

Two-photon emission is not forbidden. It happens. It just loses the race.

An excited atom decays by whatever route is fastest, and routes are ranked by how many times the electromagnetic field has to act. Emitting one photon takes one interaction. Splitting the jump into two photons takes two — and every extra interaction costs a factor of the fine-structure constant, α ≈ 1/137, in the rate.

So in an ordinary atom the one-photon route fires roughly 10⁸ times faster and the two-photon version never gets a turn. But when symmetry forbids the single-photon jump — hydrogen's metastable 2s state is the textbook case — two-photon emission is all that remains, and you see it.

And here is the lovely tell: two photons sharing one fixed energy budget can split it any way they like. So single-photon decay gives you a sharp line, and two-photon decay gives you a smooth continuum that sums, pair by pair, to exactly the same gap.

Are LEDs made with phosphors? And what is a phosphor?

Gytis, then Noa

A phosphor is a colour launderer. It is a transparent crystal — a host — deliberately contaminated with a trace of some other ion, the activator. The host does nothing but hold the ion still and set its surroundings; the activator does the work. Yttrium aluminium garnet with a pinch of cerium (YAG:Ce) is the workhorse.

Feed it a high-energy photon and it swallows it, quietly loses a bit of the energy as heat shaking the crystal, then re-emits a lower-energy one. Always downhill — that loss is called the Stokes shift, and it is why a phosphor turns blue into yellow and never the reverse.

Crucially the output is broad, not a line. A lone atom in a vacuum has knife-edge levels; an ion locked in a vibrating crystal has its levels smeared by that vibration into a wide band. That smearing is the whole point — it is how you get something that looks like a colour rather than a laser.

So: are all LEDs phosphor LEDs? No. A red, green, blue, or amber indicator LED is bare semiconductor, emitting directly, no phosphor at all. But essentially every white LED is a blue chip plus phosphor — because there is no such thing as a white-emitting semiconductor junction.

The catch is that yellow band has a hole in the deep red, which is exactly why cheap LED light makes skin and food look grey. The fix is a second phosphor, a red nitride, added to fill it in. That is the difference you are paying for when a bulb advertises high CRI.

And the same trick, one generation older, is sitting in every fluorescent tube: an argon-and-mercury discharge that emits mostly invisible ultraviolet, coating the inside of the glass with phosphor to convert that UV down into visible light. The glowing thing you see is the coating, not the gas.

So what is actually in each tube?

A field guide for the walk home

SourceMechanismSpectrum
Neon Gas discharge, pure neon ~12 lines, 585–703 nm. Orange-red only. No filter makes it another colour.
"Neon", blue Argon + a mercury droplet Mercury lines + UV. Coloured glass or phosphor sets the final hue — this is how signs get every colour that isn't red.

Fluorescent Mercury discharge → UV → phosphor Broad phosphor bands with mercury's sharp lines poking through. A hybrid.
White LED Blue junction → phosphor A blue spike plus a broad yellow hill, with a red dip unless a second phosphor fills it.

Sharp lines mean isolated atoms taking specific steps. Smooth hills mean something hot, or something vibrating. Every light you will look at tonight is one, the other, or a deliberate mixture.
